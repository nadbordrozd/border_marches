# Concept: magic, factions and the combo system

Status: Concept draft for user review (bead `border_marches-o95`).

A concept, not a spec: no spell lists, stat tables or balance numbers.
Every name and example is illustrative. Nothing here changes the MVP rules
in [GAME_DESIGN.md](GAME_DESIGN.md) yet. Heroes of Might and Magic 1-4 and
Slay the Spire are references for feel and structure; the content is our
own.

## 1. The pitch

A hero sets out across a land where whole worlds have washed up: dinosaurs
on one hill, Martians in the next valley, a gingerbread county beyond. The
land is full of spells, strange creatures and artifacts, far more than one
game can show. Battles are exact: every number is shown before it happens.
The fun is in finding that two things nobody designed together do
something outrageous, and that the rules let you keep it.

Five ideas carry this:

1. **One grammar.** Every spell, skill, artifact and creature ability is
   built from the same few nouns and verbs, so any piece can meet any
   other.
2. **Three exploitable laws.** Requirements are checked once; effects bind
   a stack by identity; anything can target anything.
3. **A five-school wheel that pays out.** Allied schools make each other's
   effects last. Opposed schools can burn each other's effects as fuel for
   one doubled spell.
4. **Factions share the grammar and, in my recommendation, the spellbook,
   and each keeps one rule only it plays by.** This is undecided;
   section 4 sets out the options.
5. **Strong tricks can turn up in the first week,** by luck. Prices are
   paid on the adventure map: mana across an expedition, creatures lost
   for good, and spells that can be cast once per battle.

## 2. A taste: the scapegoat who signed nothing

This is our descendant of the Hypnotize plus Martyr trick, and it uses
week-one pieces.

**Pieces.** _Notaries_ are caretaker paper golems I hired from a tollhouse
near home. Once per battle they stamp an enemy stack below a size limit, and it
fights for me until the round ends. _Scapegoat_ is a first-tier spell:
choose two friendly stacks; for three rounds, damage to the first is
carried to the second instead. My army is Lost World.

1. Round 2: the Notaries stamp 8 enemy Ogres. They are mine for the round.
2. On my Triceratops' turn the hero casts Scapegoat: Triceratops
   protected, Ogres as the scapegoat. This is legal, because right now
   both are friendly. A line appears between them.
3. The Ogres hit their old friends, the round ends and they turn back. The
   link stays. It was checked once, and it names the Ogres, whoever
   commands them.
4. Round 3: my Triceratops charge the Trolls. The Trolls' retaliation
   lands on the Ogres. So does everything else that hits the Triceratops
   for the next two rounds. The preview reads: "Trolls strike Triceratops
   140 → link → Ogres take 140 → 1 Ogre dies".

**Why it feels clever.** Scapegoat is meant to cost me one of my own
stacks. I found the one-round window in which an enemy could be made to
sign, and the contract outlived the signature. Nobody wrote "Notary plus
Scapegoat"; it follows from two laws.

**What it costs.** One Notary use, one cheap spell, no losses. It works
against any guard, because retaliation is automatic. It ends when the
Ogres die, and damage they cannot absorb comes back to my stack.

A second taste, with no spell at all: the cursed _Albatross_ gives my hero
a large bonus but jinxes my strongest stack in every battle. A stray
_Bellhop_ can carry one condition from a stack beside it to another. So
each battle the Bellhops carry the jinx to the enemy's best stack, and I
keep the bonus (section 8.4).

## 3. The world: the Marches

**The premise.** The Marches are a lost-and-found for worlds. Long ago a
people called the **Keepers** built five great engines to catch worlds at
the moment they were ending and set them down here, whole: a plateau of
dinosaurs from a world that sank, a crater of red sand from a dying Mars, a
gingerbread county from a story nobody tells any more, a graveyard that
kept its dead. Each arrival is a **landfall**. The Keepers are gone. The
engines still run, unattended, and worlds keep arriving. Everyone here is a
rescued castaway who does not know it, squabbling over the hills between.
The tone is cheerful and pulpy.

**The mystery.** Where did the Keepers go, and who decides what gets saved
now? Campaign maps answer this in pieces.

**The five engines are the five schools of magic.** Each did one job of
the rescue, and each still leaks its force into the land.

| School     | The engine's job                                  | On the map                                           | Its caretakers                   |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| **Bloom**  | Keep the rescued alive and fed                    | Overgrown gardens, orchards that fruit creatures     | Groundskeepers: walking hedges   |
| **Accord** | Make strangers able to live side by side          | Moot stones and tollhouses where anyone can bargain  | Notaries: stamped paper golems   |
| **Clock**  | Hold arriving worlds in order until there is room | Dials; stopped places where a battle hangs mid-swing | Winders: brass key-bearers       |
| **Hush**   | Let finished things end, and salvage the rest     | Quiet yards, ossuary libraries                       | Sextons: polite, hooded, patient |
| **Storm**  | Haul worlds in across the gap                     | Pylons and weathercocks under permanent thunder      | Riggers: crackling line-walkers  |

The **caretakers** are the natives: the Keepers' staff, still on duty.
They guard school sites, teach spells, and form a sixth roster nobody
starts with: do an order's errands and its staff will march with you.

**Landfalls and seams.** Each landfall is a biome with a **local law**,
one sentence that applies to every battle on its soil. In graveyard earth
a corpse can be used twice. On the ice, a pushed stack slides until it
hits something. Under the pylons Storm spells are not spent. In a quiet
yard nothing can be healed. Where two landfalls meet is **seam country**:
both laws apply and the strangest sites cluster.

**Strays.** Most landfalls are tiny. A stray is a landfall of one creature
type: a noir detective, a rubber-suit sea monster, a wind-up bellhop.
Strays are the neutral guards and the recruitable oddities. Each costs one
sprite and one ability, so there can be dozens and no map shows them all.

**What you do out there.**

- **One strange thing near home.** Every start has, within two days'
  travel, a piece of a Marvel, a cursed artifact, or a hut or tollhouse of
  low-tier hirelings with a strong ability. The first week can tip a game. That is intended.
- **Scouted puzzles.** A guard shows its creatures, their abilities and
  its prize. Some punish the obvious answer: the Mirror Carp returns the
  first spell cast on it. An odd guard before a known prize is an
  invitation to go and find the answer.
- **Sites that give.** Shrines and caretakers teach their spell to every
  visitor. A spell in a school I lack is never a dead find: it becomes a
  **scroll**, one use, castable by anyone, or tradeable at a tollhouse.
- **Vaults that choose.** The deepest sites show three treasures and let
  me carry out one. Drafts are for the top end only.
- **Broken Marvels.** The greatest artifacts arrive in three pieces across
  a map. A piece alone is useful, or an awkward curse. Rumours from
  tollhouses and Sextons say where the rest lie. Assembling one is the
  long quest of a game.
- **The engines.** A large map holds one or two. Reaching one grants that
  school's unique capstone for that map.
- **Adventure magic.** Bloom grows a path through forest. Accord parleys a
  stray guard into standing aside. Clock borrows tomorrow's movement.
  Hush asks the dead what lies under the fog. Storm rides the tow line
  between two pylons I have visited.

## 4. The magic structure

### 4.1 The open question: shared magic or faction magic

The more each faction runs on its own mechanic, the less a common
spellbook and a five-school wheel make sense. Three real options, each
shown on the Martians.

**A. Fully shared.** One grammar, one spellbook. A faction is a
preference: a kind of effect it makes and uses best. _Martians:_
mind-control specialists whose saucers turn weak stacks; anyone with the
Accord school does much the same, and saucers serve in any army. _Gain:_
every find is usable by every hero; the most cross-faction combos. _Cost:_
by mid-game armies and spellbooks converge and it is hard to say who you
are fighting.

**B. Shared grammar and book, plus a birthright.** As A, but each faction
has one rule only it plays by, carried by its hero classes and native
creatures, and visible on the board within a round. _Martians:_ the
**Leash**. Everyone else's mind control lasts a round. A Martian saucer
may keep a beam on its puppet and hold it for as long as the saucer does
nothing else and is not struck. A saucer hired into a caveman army still
turns a stack, for one round. _Gain:_ the treasure cave and the wheel
survive, and a spectator can name the faction from one round of play.
_Cost:_ birthrights are the least bounded part and need the most testing.

**C. Private engines on a shared grammar.** Each faction has its own
resource and its own book; only a small common pool is shared. _Martians:_
no mana and no schools. They charge Power Cells each round and spend them
on Gadgets (heat ray, tractor beam, shield) only they can use. Gadgets
still make ordinary conditions, so Martian _creatures_ combine with
everyone's. _Gain:_ the strongest flavour; each faction plays like a
different Slay the Spire character. _Cost:_ most spell finds are dead to
most heroes, and hero-side combos are scripted inside each book. The wheel
covers only the factions that use it.

**My recommendation is B.** In my judgement the treasure cave and
unscripted combos both need a common book, and a birthright borrows C's
strangeness, one private rule each, without splitting it. This is a
judgement call about which loss hurts more. The grammar is shared under every option, so the choice can
be made per faction: B can hold one or two factions built as in C
(Martians and the Deepforge are the natural ones).

### 4.2 The five schools and the wheel

```text
              Bloom
       Storm         Accord
          Hush     Clock
```

Neighbours are allied; the two schools across are opposed. Each school
owns a kind of effect, and one special tool for handling **conditions**
(the named effects that sit on a stack: Venomed, Burning, Frozen, a link,
a debt).

| School | Owns                                                | Its condition tool (second tier)                           |
| ------ | --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Bloom  | Healing, summoning, effects that grow each round    | Feed: make a condition bigger                              |
| Accord | Links between stacks, mind control, guarding, swaps | Share: copy a condition along a link                       |
| Clock  | Turn order, extra and delayed turns, durations      | Wind: advance or rewind a condition's clock                |
| Hush   | Corpses, drain, debts, switching abilities off      | Pass on: move a harmful condition to an adjacent stack     |
| Storm  | Area damage, fire, cold, pushing                    | Set off: end a condition for damage equal to what was left |

Every condition has a school and shows its colour, including those made
by creatures: Venom is Bloom, Frozen is Storm, a stamped stack is Accord,
a debt is Hush. Every tool works on every school's conditions. The wheel
then adds two options when a hero casts:

- **Echo (allied, free).** Add one round to a condition of an allied
  school on a stack the spell touches. A second school keeps the first
  one's work going.
- **Fuel (opposed, once per battle).** Burn one condition of an opposed
  school on a stack the spell touches. The condition ends and the spell is
  cast at double size.

Allies make things last; opposites make things big. Nothing is cancelled
unless the caster chooses, so effects from opposed schools can stand side
by side, and a hero with three schools has more options, not more
conflict. Any condition will do as fuel, friend's or foe's, good or bad:
Venom from my own Spitters, a curse on my own unit, an enemy's blessing
(which makes Fuel a dispel that pays). Fuel is easy to find; the
once-per-battle limit is what makes it a decision. How strong and how
frequent it should be is an open decision (section 10).

**Seam spells**, found only in seam country, need two opposed schools and
break a rule between them: _Controlled Burn_ (Storm and Accord) is an area
blast that every linked stack ignores.

### 4.3 Spells and their price

- The book is big: on the order of 25 spells per school plus seam spells.
  A map seeds about a third. Town guilds teach their faction's leanings;
  the rest is out in the land. Higher tiers need rank in the school's
  skill, and a hero holds six skills, so no hero casts everything they
  find.
- The hero stays off the field and casts **one spell per round**, on any
  friendly stack's turn.
- **Each spell is a page, and a page is spent until the battle ends.** A
  five-round battle uses five different spells, so a wide book beats a
  narrow one and the best spell is available once.
- **Mana is an expedition budget.** It returns slowly on the road and
  fully in a town with a guild.
- **Creature casters** cast pages from the same book once per battle.
  **Scrolls** are one-use pages anyone can cast, condition tools included.
  A scroll of Pass On in a week-one chest is a real event.
- **Losses are permanent.** A plan that spends my own creatures is paid
  for in gold and a week's growth.

## 5. The interaction grammar

This is the heart of the design. The fine print is in the appendix; this
section is the idea.

**The pieces on the board.** Stacks. Conditions on stacks, each with a
clock counting rounds. Links, which are conditions that join two stacks
and are drawn as lines. Ground: one feature per hex, such as fire, ice,
brambles, a corpse or sweets. And the timeline: the turn order, shown as a
bar, with scheduled events on it. Every effect in the game does something
to one of these: strike, harm, heal, move a stack, add or remove a
condition or ground, summon, or change the timeline. The MVP abilities
already fit: Venom is "on strike, add Venomed".

**The laws.**

1. **One vocabulary.** New content may only use those pieces and verbs. A
   birthright bends one rule; it does not add a subsystem.
2. **Conditions are things.** A condition can be moved, copied,
   lengthened, shortened or set off, whatever it is. Nobody writes "works
   with Venom"; they write "move a harmful condition".
3. **Checked once.** A requirement about who may be chosen (friendly,
   adjacent, living) is checked when the effect is created and never
   again.
4. **Bound by identity, sides read live.** An effect that names a stack
   keeps naming it, whoever commands it. Words like "friendly" are read at
   the moment the effect acts: a stamped Shaman's War Drums help my
   stacks.
5. **Anything can target anything.** Heal the enemy, bomb your own
   expendables, put a debt on your own stack.
6. **Death leaves ground.** Corpses, wrecks or sweets, for either side to
   use.
7. **Reactions resolve one at a time, in a fixed visible order.**

**Three safeguards** (details in the appendix). Attacks by stacks
(**Strikes**) use the damage formula and its caps; all other damage
(**Harm**) is a plain number that can never be sized by a stack's hit
points. A size limit on a condition travels with it, so mind control
cannot be laundered through a weak stack onto a strong one. And effects
that stop a stack acting are short and cannot be prolonged, so no stack is
silenced more than half the time.

**Why combos emerge.** Each condition and each kind of ground is a meeting
point. Designers write **makers** and **users** separately and never write
pairs. Frozen has an Ice Folk breath, a Storm spell and an artifact as
makers. Its users are any Strike (the first Strike on a Frozen stack is
doubled and thaws it), a dwarven hammer that triples instead, and any
caster who wants fuel. Add the five condition tools, Echo, Fuel and laws 3
to 5, and the pairs and triples outnumber anything we could script. The
discipline: a new condition needs three makers and three users across two
factions or schools before it is allowed in.

**How it stays readable.** There are no rolls, so the game can show
everything.

- **The preview is a rehearsal.** Hovering an action runs the real engine
  and shows the chain step by step, as in section 2. The player has to
  notice an opportunity; the game does the arithmetic.
- **Enemy intents.** Enemy stacks show what they will do on their turn as
  the board stands, as in Slay the Spire.
- **A plan line.** I may queue the rest of my side's round, see the
  result, then commit or take it back.
- Everything persistent is drawn on the board, every keyword is one
  sentence, and easy fights can be auto-resolved with the exact outcome
  shown first.

## 6. Growth and treasure

### 6.1 The hero

**Every level** gives a stat by the class's pattern (Attack, Defense,
Power, Lore) and a choice of one of three: a new skill or a rank in a held
skill. Offers are random, weighted by class and by schools held (allies
up, opposites down), with one offer always advancing something I have.

**Skills.** About 24, three ranks each; a hero holds six: school skills
that unlock tiers, army skills written in the grammar (widen auras by a
hex), and journey skills for movement, scouting and adventure magic.

**Feats: every third level.** A draft of three single-rule sentences from
a large pool, weighted by my skills; six or seven in a game. Some widen:
"The first harmful condition you place each battle also lands on a stack
adjacent to the target." "Fuel triples instead of doubling." Many are
**vows**, which narrow in exchange for breaking a rule:

- "Your Hush pages are not spent when cast on your own stacks or on
  corpses. You cannot cast Bloom."
- "You may cast twice in the first round. You never cast after round
  three."
- "Your scrolls are not used up; each still costs mana and is spent once
  per battle like a page. You hold no school above rank 1."

Vows are how a hero commits, and how two heroes with the same book play
differently.

**Class.** Two per faction. A class sets the stat pattern, the offer
weights, the birthright and a **Command**: a once-per-battle act that is
not a spell and grows with level, so a fighting hero is felt on the field.
A Chieftain's _Stampede_: every friendly stack adjacent to one enemy
strikes it at once.

**Chance and steering.** As in Slay the Spire, the player recognises a
build in what the map offers: the strange thing near home, an off-school
shrine, a hut of strays. A few sites let me steer: a hermit who unteaches
a skill, a rumour that names a vault's contents.

**Builds this should produce:** the link-weaver whose key stack cannot be
hurt; the undertaker who ends battles with more stacks than they brought;
the debtor who takes power on credit and never pays; the arsonist who
sets their own army alight for fuel; the scroll-hoarder with no school at
all; the one great stack with every blessing Echoed to last.

### 6.2 Artifacts

| Grade       | What it does                                                         | Where                                                            |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Trinket** | A modest plain bonus                                                 | Everywhere                                                       |
| **Wonder**  | Makes or uses a condition or ground, or joins two with a trigger     | Guarded sites from the first week; several per game              |
| **Marvel**  | Breaks one core rule in one sentence: a law from its lost home world | One to three per map, from dozens; many in pieces, one near home |

Wonders:

- _Winter's Pocket Watch_: the fastest enemy stack starts each battle
  Frozen.
- _Sexton's Spade_: corpses of either side are mine to use.
- _The Bellhop's Cap_: once per battle, a stack of mine carries one
  condition from an adjacent stack to another.
- _Tinderbox_: I may use Fuel a second time each battle.

Cursed Wonders, whose drawbacks are conditions, so the right build turns
them over:

- _The Albatross_: the hero gains a large Attack and Defense bonus. My
  strongest stack starts every battle Jinxed: it cannot retaliate and
  Strikes against it are stronger.
- _Coat of Embers_: my stacks Strike harder, and all start each battle
  Burning.

Marvels:

- _The Second Left Glove_: the hero may cast twice per round, but the
  second page must be of a school opposed to the first.
- _The Hourglass with No Bottom_: clocks on my stacks' conditions do not
  tick (Holds excepted). Blessings and debts alike.
- _Treaty Ink_: when a linked stack dies, its end of the link passes to
  the nearest stack, friend or foe.
- _The Unspent Page_: one chosen page is never spent.
- _The Understudy's Mask_: once per battle, a stack of mine uses the
  ability of any stack on the field.

A broken Marvel's pieces each carry part of the rule, sometimes the worse
part: one third of the Hourglass stops only the clocks on my harmful
conditions. Worn slots are limited, so late in a game good things stay in
the pack. A Marvel should make the player re-read their whole book.

### 6.3 Creatures

- Each creature has one or two abilities, each a sentence in the grammar.
  Low tiers enable, high tiers cash in, and the top tier bends a rule
  (the T-Rex ignores retaliation).
- Strays and caretakers carry the strong verbs at low tiers, in small numbers: a
  **Bellhop** carries a condition, a **Notary** turns a stack for a round,
  a **Winder** delays a stack (a Clock condition) or advances or rewinds
  one clock.
- An army is mostly natives with one or two **guests**. Towns grow only
  natives, off-faction dwellings are few, and a birthright works only for
  natives. Guests keep their abilities. They are the splash that makes the
  build.

## 7. Faction sketches

Nine factions. Leanings are uneven on purpose: a faction leans where its
fiction points. Each birthright is a rule a spectator can see within a
round. Under option C the birthright column would grow into a private
engine; under option A it would go.

| Faction              | Genre, leaning                       | Feel                                         | Birthright                                                                                                       | Examples                |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| The Lost World       | Cavemen and dinosaurs; Bloom         | A pack closing in                            | **The Pack**: when a native strikes, every other native beside the target nips it too.                           | Spitters, T-Rex         |
| The Sweetlands       | Fairy-tale confection; Bloom         | Feeding the front line to the heavy hitters  | **Edible**: a native may eat an adjacent Sweet stack whole, gaining a burst of speed, healing and its blessings. | Gumdrop Guard, Jelly    |
| The Vowed            | Storybook chivalry; Accord           | One body, until a link is cut                | **Sworn pairs**: natives deploy already linked in pairs; partners share healing and may swap places.             | Squires, Questing Beast |
| The Martians         | Saucer invasion; Accord, some Storm  | Winning with other people's armies           | **The Leash**: a saucer may hold its puppet for as long as it does nothing else and is not struck.               | Brain Saucers, Tripods  |
| The Deepforge        | Steam-age dwarves; Clock, some Storm | Planning two rounds ahead and being right    | **Banked turns**: a native that passes stores the turn and may later act twice in a row.                         | Mortars, Clockwinders   |
| The Ice Folk         | Polar adventure; Storm, some Clock   | The enemy's turns keep not happening         | **Curling**: natives can push a Frozen stack; it slides, and it strikes what it hits.                            | Snow Slingers, Mammoths |
| The Restless         | Graveyard comedy; Hush               | Losing three rounds and winning the battle   | **Not done yet**: a native stack that dies gets up from its corpse at the next round's start, smaller, once.     | Gravecallers, Late King |
| The Cult of the Deep | Weird-tale horror; Hush, some Accord | Juggling debts                               | **Offerings**: the hero's pages are never spent; casting a page again costs creatures.                           | Acolytes, Bell-Ringers  |
| The Goblin Horde     | Sword-and-sorcery rabble; Storm      | Mutual destruction, survived slightly better | **Launched**: a native caught in its own side's blast is unharmed and thrown three hexes from the centre.        | Bomb Chuckers, Warboss  |

Goblins become a faction; strays and caretakers take over as neutrals.
Nothing raised in battle survives it, for any faction: the necromancy
snowball of older games is left out, and the Restless get their numbers
back inside the fight instead: a stack that got up crumbles when the
battle ends.

## 8. More worked combos

With section 2, these are five that work and one that fails. Each states
its price in turns, mana and losses.

### 8.1 Everybody's weather but mine (opposed schools: Storm and Accord)

Continuing from section 2, with the link still up. My Triceratops stand
among three enemy stacks, the Ogres included.

1. The hero casts _Broadside_ (Storm): heavy Harm to every stack in an
   area, mine included.
2. The Triceratops' share goes down the link. The Ogres take two shares
   and my stack takes none.
3. The link is Accord and Broadside is Storm; nothing cancels, so the
   link stands. Next round my guest Bomb Chuckers can do it again.
4. To go bigger, I spend my one Fuel. My Winders earlier slowed the
   Trolls, which is a Clock condition, opposed to Storm. I burn it and
   Broadside lands at double on all three.

**Why it feels clever.** A spell I could never cast into my own melee
becomes my best page, and the enemy pays my share.

**Price.** Two pages over two rounds, the Notaries' one use, a turn from
the Winders, the battle's Fuel. No losses.

### 8.2 Burning the bill (opposed schools: Hush and Bloom)

_Acolytes_ are Cult creatures with Dark Bargain: a friendly stack strikes
harder for three rounds and carries a Reckoning, a Hush debt. Every point
of bonus damage is added to it, and it lands on the bearer as Harm when
the clock runs out. _Regrow_ (Bloom) restores creatures a stack lost this
battle.

1. Round 1: the Acolytes bargain with my Raptors.
2. Rounds 1 to 3: the Raptors hit at the bonus and take losses doing it.
   The debt grows, visibly.
3. Round 3, before it falls due: the hero casts Regrow on the Raptors and
   burns the Reckoning as Fuel. The debt is gone and Regrow is doubled:
   the Raptors are back at full count.

**The other ending.** With _Pass On_ (the Hush tool) instead, the
Raptors step beside the enemy Warbosses and the hero passes the bill to
them. A scroll or a Bellhop does the same without the school.

**Why it feels clever.** The Cult's whole design is that somebody pays.
Either nobody does, or the enemy does.

**Price.** One page, the battle's Fuel or adjacency at the right moment,
and guest Acolytes. This is not a rare build: any hero with Acolytes and
one Bloom or Accord page can do it every battle. The bonus damage went
through Strikes and kept their caps, so it is strong, not unbounded.

### 8.3 The undertaker's engine (a whole build)

Hush rank 2 for _Second Shift_ (raise a corpse as a skeleton stack). A
feat: ground I consume returns a little mana. _Sexton's Spade_. The vow
that keeps Hush pages cast on my own stacks or on corpses. Gravecallers,
who raise once per battle themselves. Goblins split into five small
stacks.

1. Round 1: the goblin stacks run in and die beside the enemy line.
2. Every round the hero raises a corpse. The vow keeps the page; the feat
   returns part of the mana.
3. Skeletons stand next to shooters (who can no longer shoot) and next to
   my Raptors' targets (feeding their pack bonus).
4. Enemy dead become mine through the Spade.

**Why it feels clever.** It is an economy assembled over a whole game
from a skill, a feat, a vow, a Wonder and two creatures.

**Price.** The goblins are gone for good. A corpse is used once; raised
stacks leave none and vanish after the battle; one cast per round.

### 8.4 The albatross changes necks (an early find)

_The Albatross_ lies two days from my town; _Bellhops_ live in a hut on
the way.

1. Every battle, my T-Rex starts Jinxed.
2. Round 2: the T-Rex reaches the enemy's best stack. The Bellhops,
   beside both, carry the jinx across.
3. For the rest of the battle that stack cannot retaliate and takes
   stronger Strikes. I keep the Albatross's bonus.

**Why it feels clever.** The item's price was written as a condition, and
conditions are things. No spell, no skill, week one.

**Price.** The Bellhops' one carry per battle, and a round and a half in
which a Jinxed T-Rex crosses the field. It works every fight. That is an
early big advantage, as intended, and it is also a
routine; section 10 asks what to do about routines.

### 8.5 One that fails: laundering a mind

The Notaries stamp a weak goblin stack next to the enemy T-Rex, and I
cast Pass On to pass the stamp to the T-Rex. The preview refuses:
the stamp carries its size limit, and the T-Rex exceeds it. The right
play shows in the same preview: wear the T-Rex down under the limit
first, and then the pass works.

## 9. What may break, and how we test it

**Allowed to be broken.** A first week that tips the game. One stack
untouchable for a couple of rounds. A puppet held all battle on a Leash.
A far larger stack killed in one action when four pieces line up. A debt
never paid. A Marvel that rewrites the plan. A guard AI with no answer.
Endless loops, battles that cannot end and boards the preview cannot
explain are never allowed (appendix).

**Policy.** If an exploit hangs the game, ends it outright or cannot be
previewed, fix it. If it only wins, keep it and consider making it rarer.

**The proof step.** Before building breadth, test the bet with the
smallest slice that could falsify it: three schools forming two allied
pairs and one opposed pair (Bloom, Storm, Hush); three factions whose
sprites exist (the Lost World, the Goblins, the Restless) with their
birthrights; about thirty spells including three condition tools; ten
artifacts, two cursed; six strays. First on paper: draw three random
pieces and ask whether they interact. If most draws do nothing, the
vocabulary is too wide or too narrow.

## Appendix: fine print and limits

For checking the combos; not needed to judge the concept.

**Stacks.** A stack has an owner (never changes) and a side (the owner's,
unless Turned). A side loses when it owns no living stacks and none
waiting to get up (the Restless birthright). Battles have a round limit.

**Strike and Harm.** A Strike is an attack by a stack: melee, shot,
retaliation or a delayed shell. It uses the damage formula and its caps,
and only a Strike triggers "on strike" and "on being struck". Harm is a
plain number: spell damage, Burning, a debt, damage carried by a link. It
ignores Defense and triggers nothing but death. Harm is sized by the
hero's Power, a stack's count or damage already dealt, never by hit
points.

**Links.** A link is a condition on the protected stack; the far end is
only named. A Strike on the protected stack is computed against it,
retaliation included; the number travels down the link as Harm; what the
far end cannot absorb comes back.

**What a condition carries.** Moved or copied, it keeps its size, clock,
school, whether it is a boon or a bane, and its gate. A gate is a visible
threshold in place of a resist roll, tested on every stack the condition
lands on. Requirements about choosing (law 3) are not re-tested. A
condition never stacks with itself; growing ones have a maximum.

**Spells.** A spell is a page cast by a hero, a creature caster or a
scroll. It touches every stack it chooses or catches in its area; a
condition tool touches both giver and receiver. A creature's own ability
is not a spell, but the conditions it makes have schools. Echo and Fuel
are chosen by a casting hero and resolve before the spell, using
conditions already present. Fuel never doubles a Hold's clock.

**Turned.** "This stack fights for the side opposite its owner's."
Turning a Turned stack is refused. Turned is a bane and can be passed on,
gate permitting; passed to my own stack, that stack defects. A stack
summoned by a Turned caster belongs to the side it was summoned for.

**Holds.** Frozen and Asleep stop a stack acting; Turned takes its
loyalty. A Hold lasts at most two rounds. It can be moved, ended or burnt
as fuel, never lengthened, rewound, copied or doubled. When it leaves,
the stack refuses Holds for as many rounds as it was held. For Frozen and
Asleep nothing overrides this. Turned may be bent by a birthright, since
a Turned stack still acts and the battle still ends.

**Reactions.** Each fires once per action, in this order: the acting
stack's, the target's, then everyone else's in timeline order.

**Clutter caps.** One feature per hex, six conditions per stack, one link
of each kind per stack. Creature tags decide what a death leaves; a tag
may also make a stack ineligible for at most two named conditions.
Otherwise there are no immunities.

**Bounded.** One hero cast per round, two with a rule-breaker. One Fuel
per battle unless an item or feat says otherwise. One extra turn per
stack per round. Summoned and raised stacks leave no ground and do not
outlive the battle.

**Forbidden.** Every repeatable effect consumes something finite (mana,
ground, creatures, pages, rounds) and none refunds its full cost. No
hidden state, nothing the rehearsal cannot show, no keyword longer than a
sentence.

**Left for fleshing out.** Whether a Pack nip is a full Strike; what
Offerings accepts as payment; split saucers and several Leashes; what
Fuel doubles on a summon or a link; who chooses the battleground; whether
the AI uses Echo and Fuel; all numbers.
